The Limit Order Book

flowchart TD A["ASK 100.04 x500"] --> B["ASK 100.03 x200"] B --> C["ASK 100.02 x300 best ask"] C -. "spread 0.02" .- D["BID 100.00 x400 best bid"] D --> E["BID 99.99 x700"] E --> F["BID 99.98 x250"]

Two priority queues facing across the spread. Bids stacked high→low, asks low→high; best of each = top of book (L1). Depth = resting qty per level. A limit order adds liquidity & waits; a marketable order crosses and takes.

  • Price-time priority (FIFO): rank by price, then arrival time. Earliest at a level fills first → queue position is alpha.
  • Pro-rata: split incoming across a level proportional to size (rewards being big, not early).
  • Big market order walks the book across levels at worse prices — avg fill ≠ touch !
  • Modify (size up / reprice) usually loses time priority → back of queue !

LOB as a Data Structure

Naive single sorted container = O(log n)/op, cache-hostile. Real engines: array/hashmap of price levels, each level a FIFO queue.

OpTargetHow
addO(1)push tail of level queue
cancelO(1)intrusive linked list, direct node handle
executeO(1) amortpop head, decrement
best bid/askO(1)cached ptr / bitmap of active levels
  • Intrusive doubly-linked list: next/prev live in the order node → unlink in O(1) given the handle (no search). Cancels dominate flow, so this is the point !
  • Prices are discrete (ticks) → index levels by (price-base)/tick into a flat array; hot levels stay cache-resident.
  • Order-id → node map for cancel/modify lookup.

Spread & Liquidity

Quoted spread = ask − bid. Effective spread = 2×|price − mid| (what you actually paid). Realized spread = effective minus the price move after → the MM's true take.

Three components of the spread

ComponentCompensates for
Order-processingfixed cost of making a market (tech, ops)
Inventory-holdingprice risk of carrying non-zero position
Adverse-selectionlosses to informed counterparties !
  • Liquidity = tightness (spread), depth (size), resiliency (recovery speed). Not one number.
  • Tick size floors the spread; a large min tick → wide queues, queue-position games. Sub-penny prohibited (Rule 612) for ≥$1.00 stocks.

Price Formation Models

ModelCore idea
Roll (1984)infer effective spread from negative serial covariance of price changes (bid-ask bounce): s = 2√(−cov)
Glosten-Milgromspread arises purely from adverse selection; bid/ask = conditional expectations of value given a sell/buy
Kyle (1985)informed trader hides in noise; impact linear in flow, Δp = λ·q. Kyle's λ = illiquidity coefficient
  • Key: even with zero inventory & processing cost, a spread must exist to break even against informed flow (Glosten-Milgrom).
  • Price is a martingale only conditional on public info; the bounce makes raw prints mean-revert at the tick ! (bounce ≠ signal).
  • Kyle's λ is the empirical bridge to the square-root impact law.

Market Making

Continuously quote two-sided; earn the spread on the round trip. Spread is compensation, not free margin — it pays for inventory risk + adverse selection.

The two costs that eat the spread

  • Inventory risk: fills arrive asymmetric → you carry position q; variance of loss ~ σ²·time. Ideal end-of-day inventory = 0.
  • Adverse selection: your resting ask fills exactly when fair value jumps above it → you sold cheap ! Defense: widen / pull / cancel fast.

Two distinct levers !

LeverMovesControls
Skewcenter of quotesinventory (long → shift both down to sell)
Widentotal widthvolatility / toxicity
Flat  q=0:    bid 99.98 / ask 100.02
Long  q=+500: bid 99.96 / ask 100.00   (skew DOWN)

Speed is adverse-selection insurance: cancel a stale quote before informed flow hits it.

Avellaneda-Stoikov

Center quotes on the inventory-adjusted reservation price, not the mid:

r = s - q * gamma * sigma^2 * (T - t)

s     mid / fair value       q     signed inventory
gamma risk aversion          sigma volatility
(T-t) time to horizon
  • Flat (q=0) → r = mid, symmetric quotes. Long (q>0) → r below mid → biased to sell.
  • More q, gamma, or σ² → skew harder. (T−t)→0 near close → skew collapses, flatten hard.

Optimal total spread

spread ~ gamma*sigma^2*(T-t)          # inventory term
       + (2/gamma)*ln(1 + gamma/kappa) # fill/market term
quote at  r ± spread/2

! A-S assumes fixed fair value + symmetric flow; real markets have trends, informed flow, queue dynamics it ignores. Know where it breaks.

Systems Hot Path — Kill Jitter

Determinism over throughput. The enemy is variance, not average. Remove every jitter source on the critical path.

Jitter sourceFix
alloc / GCno alloc on hot path; pools; C++/Rust not Java/Py
page faultspre-fault + mlock; huge pages
cache/TLB missSoA layout, warm cache, huge pages
NUMA cross-socketpin thread and memory to one node
IRQ / ctx switchisolcpus, pin, busy-poll, IRQs off core
C-states / freq scalingdisable; fixed clock

Lock-free SPSC queue between stages

// single-producer / single-consumer ring
struct alignas(64) Slot { T v; };   // pad = no false sharing
std::atomic<size_t> head, tail;      // separate cache lines
// no mutex: producer writes tail, consumer reads head
  • False sharing ! two hot vars on one 64B line → cores ping-pong the line. Fix: alignas(64) / pad.
  • SPSC beats a mutex queue: no syscall, no contention, wait-free.

Market Data & Feeds

A feed is a change-log, not a database ! — the exchange sends add/cancel/execute events; you replay them to build the book locally.

TermMeaning
Direct feedraw per-venue binary multicast (Nasdaq ITCH); fastest, build book yourself
SIPconsolidated public NBBO; complete but slower (extra hop) !
L1/L2/L3top-of-book / aggregated depth / per-order (only L3 gives queue position)
OUCHNasdaq order-entry protocol (outbound)
  • Reconstruction: sync to a snapshot, then apply increments from its sequence number on.
  • Seq numbers detect gaps (n+2 after n = lost msg → a dropped log entry corrupts the book forever) !
  • A/B line arbitration: two identical feeds, take first packet per seq, dedup — UDP multicast has no free retransmit.
  • PTP (IEEE 1588) HW timestamps, sub-us; NTP (ms) is useless for tick-to-trade !

Market Impact & TCA

Impact = the adverse move your own trading causes; for a big order it dwarfs commissions.

ComponentNature
Temporaryconsumed liquidity; recovers as book refills → rewards slicing
Permanentinfo leakage; level stays shifted → scales with net size

Square-root law

impact ~ Y * sigma * sqrt(Q / V)
Q order size · V volume · Y~O(1) · Q/V participation
  • Concave, not linear ! 2× size → √2≈1.41× impact. Cost/share rises, total grows sub-linearly.
  • Almgren-Chriss: minimize E[cost] + lambda·Var[cost]. Risk-neutral (λ=0) → trade slow; risk-averse → front-load. The impact-vs-timing-risk optimum.
  • TCA grades vs a benchmark: arrival (IS), interval VWAP. Beating VWAP while missing arrival can still be a bad alpha execution !

Order-Flow Signals

At ms horizons the mid is weakly predictable from book state. Prediction is easy; beating cost is hard.

imbalance  = bid_size / (bid_size + ask_size)   # >0.5 leans UP
microprice = (bid*ask_size + ask*bid_size)
             / (bid_size + ask_size)
  • OFI (order-flow imbalance): net buy vs sell pressure from book changes; one of the most robust short-horizon predictors.
  • Microprice weights toward the thin side (cross-weighting) — that's where price is heading. Not the heavy side !
  • Big bid = support → next move up (heavy side wins), not "sellers will hit it".
  • Queue position is itself alpha: front fills first and is less adversely selected.
  • Net alpha must beat fees + spread + impact ! a 0.3-tick prediction can't pay a 1-tick spread. Capture cheaply via passive/queue.
  • Signal decay: edge evaporates in ms — latency to act is part of whether it survives.

Statistical Arbitrage (HF)

Trade mean-reversion of a spread between related instruments; market-neutral by construction.

  • Pairs: find two cointegrated names; short the rich, long the cheap; unwind on convergence. Cointegration ≠ correlation ! (correlation of returns; cointegration = stationary spread of levels).
  • Signal: z-score of the spread; enter at ±2σ, exit near 0. Half-life of reversion sets the holding horizon.
  • Crowding / correlation breakdown is the killer ! everyone in the same trade → the "stable" relationship snaps under stress (Aug 2007 quant quake). The spread that "always reverts" can trend to a stop.
  • At HF: hundreds of micro-reversions/day; edge per trade tiny, so costs dominate — same net-alpha discipline as signals.
  • Basket / factor-neutral variants hedge sector & market betas, not just one pair.

Risk & Controls

An automated system is a firehose with no hand on the valve — safety must be automated and sit in front of every order, on the hot path.

Mandatory pre-trade checks

CheckPrevents
fat-finger price/sizeabsurd price or qty

duplicate detectionbug/retry double-send
self-trade prevention (STP)wash trades
  • SEC Rule 15c3-5 (Market Access Rule): automated pre-trade controls are legally mandatory; bans naked/unfiltered sponsored access; broker owns the gate.
  • Kill switch must cancel resting orders + block new atomically — not just kill the process (leaves live orders) !
  • Defense in depth: strategy → risk gate → firm kill switch → exchange breakers/LULD. Each assumes the layer above fails.
  • Knight Capital (2012): bad deploy reactivated dead test code → runaway router → ~$440M in ~45 min, firm dead. A deployment/process failure, not a coding bug ! Faster system → more essential controls.

Backtesting HFT — Honesty

You are re-simulating an event-driven system you participate in, not sampling a price series. Every shortcut adds false optimism.

The cardinal sin

! "Price traded through my limit, so I was filled." No — only if the volume ahead of you in the queue was fully consumed. A small print at your price may never reach you.

What you must model

  • Fill / queue position: track where you sit; front fills first, back may never fill.
  • Latency: your order arrives 5–50us late → reorders you in the queue, can flip a good passive fill into an adverse-selected one !
  • Your own impact: your order wasn't in the historical data — injecting it changes the queue & can make others fade.
  • Look-ahead bias: reacting to info in the same packet before you'd have decoded it.
  • Microstructure noise: high in-sample Sharpe often = fitting bid-ask bounce; won't persist out of sample.

Use an L3 event-driven LOB simulator; inject your orders as first-class price-time events. The simulator is the product.

High-Frequency Data & Time Series

  • Tick data is irregularly spaced in time and enormous — one liquid name is millions of events a day. You either work in event time (per trade/quote) or resample to bars; sampling in volume or dollar time (equal activity per bar) gives better statistical properties than clock time !
  • Microstructure noise: at very high frequency the observed price is the efficient price plus a bid-ask bounce, so naive realised variance explodes as sampling frequency rises. Fixes: sample sparsely (5-min was the classic), or use noise-robust estimators (two-scale / realised kernels).
  • High-frequency returns are not iid Gaussian: strong negative first-order autocorrelation from bounce, volatility clustering, fat tails, and intraday seasonality (the U-shaped volume/volatility over the day). Any model or backtest that assumes stationarity across the day is wrong !
  • Timestamps are the whole game: exchange vs gateway vs capture time can differ by microseconds, clocks drift, and you must know which one you're using or your "signal" is just clock skew.

Machine Learning & Modern HFT

  • Features come from the book and the tape: order-book imbalance, microprice, queue sizes and their changes, trade sign runs, cancellation rates, and cross-asset lead-lag. The edge is usually in feature engineering, not the model.
  • Labelling is subtle — predicting the sign of the next mid-move over a short horizon, or whether a fill will be adverse. The triple-barrier method (profit/stop/time) beats fixed-horizon labels for execution problems.
  • Overfitting is lethal because signals are weak (an information coefficient of a few percent is good) and non-stationary — regimes change intraday. Use walk-forward validation, purge/embargo around the label horizon to avoid leakage, and prefer simple, regularised models you can reason about.
  • Latency of inference matters: a model that predicts well but takes 50µs to score is useless on a 1µs path. Linear models and small trees dominate in production; heavy nets live in slower, alpha-research or execution-scheduling layers, not the hot path !

FPGA & Hardware Acceleration

  • The lowest-latency shops move the tick-to-trade path onto an FPGA: the network feed is parsed, the book updated, a simple strategy evaluated, and an order emitted entirely in hardware, in tens of nanoseconds — no CPU, no OS, no cache misses.
  • The tradeoff is flexibility and dev cost: FPGA logic (Verilog/VHDL/HLS) is hard to change, so only the simplest, most latency-critical decisions live there; anything complex stays in software with a slower budget. A common design is FPGA for the "fast path" (cancels, obvious quotes) and CPU for the "smart path".
  • NIC offload: kernel-bypass (Solarflare/Onload, DPDK) removes the OS from the loop for software paths; smart-NICs can do feed parsing and even risk checks. Deterministic (low-jitter) latency often matters more than the mean — a p99.9 spike is what gets you picked off.

Colocation & Connectivity

  • Colocation: renting rack space in the exchange's data centre so your server sits metres from the matching engine. Exchanges standardise cable lengths so no colo tenant is closer than another — the remaining edge is inside your box.
  • Between venues, propagation delay is physics: fibre is ~5µs/km and light in a straight line (microwave / millimetre-wave towers) beats fibre because it's faster through air and takes a shorter path. The Chicago–New York microwave race shaved the round trip toward the theoretical minimum.
  • The full jitter budget is a chain — NIC, kernel bypass, cache residency, branch prediction, GC-free code — and the slowest hop dominates. You optimise the tail, not the average, because adverse selection happens exactly when you're slow.

Scenario Playbook

  • "Design a market-making system": feed handler → book builder → fair-value/microprice signal → quoting engine (Avellaneda-Stoikov style skew by inventory) → risk/position limits (15c3-5 style pre-trade checks) → order gateway, all on a kill-jitter hot path, with a separate slow path for parameters and monitoring. State where the latency budget goes.
  • "Your strategy's live P&L is worse than backtest": walk the differences — fill assumptions (did you assume you got the touch?), market impact, latency/queue position, adverse selection, fees/rebates, and survivorship in the data. HFT backtests lie mostly about fills and queue position !
  • "Diagnose a latency spike": is it a GC/allocation, a cache miss, a lock, a syscall, NUMA cross-socket traffic, or network? Reproduce, measure with hardware timestamps, and attack the tail.

Queue Position & Adverse Selection

  • Queue position is the hidden alpha of passive strategies: at a price level filled FIFO, being near the front means you get filled on the good flow and can cancel before the bad; being at the back means you only fill when the price is about to move against you. Modelling your realistic queue position is the single biggest source of backtest error for a maker !
  • Adverse selection: your resting order fills precisely because someone with better information (or speed) traded against it — so passive fills are systematically "toxic". A market maker's edge is the spread minus adverse selection minus fees; the whole game is quoting wide/skewed enough that the spread wins on average.
  • Order-flow toxicity (e.g. VPIN) tries to measure how informed current flow is, so you can widen or pull quotes when toxicity spikes. During the 2010 Flash Crash, toxicity was extreme and liquidity evaporated as makers pulled — a canonical example of why static quoting is dangerous.

Regulation & Market Quality — Deeper

  • Reg NMS (US) created the order-protection rule and the NBBO, which is what makes latency arbitrage between venues profitable in the first place — you race to trade a stale quote before it updates. MiFID II (EU) added algo registration, clock-sync (RTS 25), and tighter reporting.
  • Market-quality debates: HFT tightens spreads and adds displayed liquidity in normal times, but critics point to fleeting liquidity, quote stuffing, and fragility in stress. Circuit breakers (LULD in the US), minimum resting times, and batch auctions (as IEX's speed bump popularised) are the main policy responses.
  • Pre-trade risk (SEC Rule 15c3-5, the "market access rule") legally requires fat-finger and credit checks on the path before an order reaches the exchange — the Knight Capital blow-up ($440M in 45 minutes from a bad deploy) is the standing lesson in why deploys and kill switches are life-or-death here !
